How Finance Borrowed Its Randomness
E very model used to price risk today — Value at Risk, credit-default formulas, Basel's capital requirements — rests on a single inherited assumption: that market movements are random. This article traces how that idea, borrowed from physics and formalized by Bachelier and Wiener, was built up through Black-Scholes, Merton, and Vasicek into the regulatory architecture used today, and how even the 2008 crisis left its foundations untouched.

The Probabilistic View of Economics

In the world of economics, the application of statistical models has a long and firmly established tradition. Such models are widely employed across a diverse range of economic applications, and the foundation for their use rests on an accepted and unquestioned paradigm — the randomness of the phenomena observed. It is on precisely this conceptual foundation that the financial safety system was likewise built, one sanctioned by successive regulatory accords (Basel I, Basel II, Basel III/CRR III). A key place within this architecture is occupied by risk models — the tools used for calculating the height of the anti-crisis barrier. These models, whose common denominator is the Value at Risk concept, were themselves derived precisely from this probabilistic conceptual map. Their credibility was, admittedly, shaken during the crisis events of 2008; yet, as can be gathered from the most recent post-crisis regulations proposed by supervisors, the conceptual foundations remained untouched. The idea of observing risk phenomena on the probabilistic map was not called into question.

In the sections that follow, the process will be traced by which economic — or, more broadly, socio-economic — phenomena came to be perceived in precisely this manner, as they are today. The founding idea, it would seem, was the already-mentioned concept of the random walk. Subsequently, by way of Markowitz, Samuelson, Black-Scholes, Merton, Vasicek, and J.P. Morgan, we arrived at the world of economic models we have today. These models are sanctioned by regulators, and, for the past twenty years, have officially told us the story of financial risk.

The Random Walk and the Normal Distribution

As late as the nineteenth century, the value of assets (e.g., stock prices) was understood through two traditions:

(a) The first tradition — the fundamental one. A stock price reflects the value of the company. A company's value depends on its profits, assets, and prospects. A skilled analyst, understanding the company, can predict whether the price will rise or fall.

(b) The second tradition — the technical one. Prices exhibit patterns — trends, resistance levels, support levels. A skilled observer, reading charts, can predict price movements.

The idea of the random walk — known in physics as Brownian motion — was transferred into the world of finance by Bachelier (1900). His work, however, was forgotten, and this idea is best known today under the name of the Wiener process (1923), or simply as Brownian motion. This widely disseminated concept holds that movements in asset values are, by their very nature, random, and follow a stochastic process (that is, a process composed of random variables). At any given moment in time, the value depends on the state at the previous period and on a random movement occurring within the current period. The stochastic process in question unfolds over time. Time, in turn, may be either discrete or continuous. Wiener (1923), who formalized the description of this process, addressed the process in continuous time.

Now, by means of the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), one can, at any given moment in the future, observe a distribution — a normal one — of possible values; that is, a picture of the potential for different possible trajectories to be realized. In the discrete version, however, the process needs more time to form a normal distribution — with a small sample (a short period t), the distribution will not yet be normal. If, on the other hand, continuous time is assumed, then, between the moments t0 and t1, infinitely many small steps are observed. Thus, within any arbitrarily small interval of time, the particle (the price) has taken infinitely many steps, and the CLT has already come into effect. The distribution of the particle's (the price's) position is thus normal for every t > 0. The Wiener process thus holds that the position of the particle at any given moment t is a random variable with a normal distribution. The marginal distribution of the Wiener process is written as follows:

$$ W(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t) \quad \text{for every } t > 0 $$

where W(t) is the value of the Wiener process at time t — that is, the position of the particle (the price) at time t — with the normal distribution described by the following parameters: a mean equal to 0, and a variance equal to the time t (that is, the standard deviation is the square root of the time t).

Black and Scholes (1973) applied precisely this continuous-time version of the random walk process, employing, in their option pricing formula, the heat conduction equation. In doing so, they accepted that the volatility of an asset's price over time could be described mathematically in the very same manner in which the random motion of a physical particle had been described. The distribution of such an asset price change is, accordingly, normal (Strictly speaking, this refers to the normality of the logarithm of the rate of return - geometric Brownian motion. It is useful to understand the rate of return as the change in value over time. In this sense, the normal distribution describes the logarithm of the change in stock price over time.).

The Random Walk and the Normal Distribution in Market Risk

In 1996, J.P. Morgan and Reuters (1996), in introducing the Value at Risk concept into the world of finance, placed precisely this random walk process at the foundation of their model, as the description of an asset's rate of return. From this followed the normal distribution they applied as the probabilistic description of the rate-of-return phenomenon; it was on this distribution that VaR values were read, given an assumed confidence level and time horizon. The supervisor, by way of the so-called Market Risk Amendment (BIS, BCBS, 1996), introduced this method into official use. Today, this is precisely how the world models the volatility of the market value of assets.

The Emergence of Credit Instrument Pricing Methods

In the domain of credit risk, a decisive step was taken by Merton (1974). Building on the Black-Scholes model (1973), he formulated a theory for the pricing of bonds under conditions of significant default risk. Adopting a series of simplifying assumptions — the most important being the continuity of price movements (that is, the continuous-time random walk) and the serial independence of unexpected rates of return — he derived a pricing equation describing the value of any given financial instrument as a function of firm value and time.

In this approach, only a single flow value is drawn from a given period (block) — most often a single year — namely, the maximum value; and this value, together with other such values drawn from other blocks, is subjected to statistical modeling. Adopting such an ap-proach gives rise, in a natural manner, to the concept of the T-period flood — for example, the 100-year flood, that is, a flood occurring once every T (every 100) blocks, and thus once every 100 years. This is precisely the return period.

The Assumption of Stationarity and Independence

It is self-evident within hydrology that the methods described above for determining the return period, as well as the number of events over the period T, can be applied only if two fundamental conditions are satisfied: (i) extreme events are drawn from a stationary distribution; and (ii) they are independent of one another. Both, when satisfied, are equivalent to ensuring the i.i.d. condition (independent and identically distributed) for the distribution of the phenomenon, and to ensuring the constancy of the exceedance probability p — the probability of exceeding a certain water level. Within hydrology, this is to be understood as meaning that, over the period observed (e.g., T = 100 years), we assume constant conditions — that is, an unchanging distribution and its variance (an unchanging probability p) — as well as the independence of successive observations drawn from such a flow distribution. Assumptions of this kind were formulated at the time when the scope of methods and approaches for modeling the flood phenomenon was being established. Doubts as to the validity of adopting such conditions, which were already emerging at that time within the research community, were commented on by Gumbel in 1941 in the following manner (Volpi et al., 2015):

In order to apply any theory, we have to suppose that the data are homogeneous, i.e., that no systematical change of climate and no important change in the basin have occurred within the observation period and that no such changes will take place in the period for which extrapolations are made. It is only under these obvious conditions that forecasts can be made.

(Volpi et al., 2015)

The hydrological models that have been in operation over the past hundred years in the United States, as well as the models widely applied in other countries, have thus been based on i.i.d. assumptions. This was an assumption that was necessary, and one not subject to discussion.

Parametrizations of the Return Period in the World of Physical Phenomena

Within the domain of physical phenomena, researchers and the institutions engaged in describing them have developed various calibrations of the return period, each useful in the processes for which they are applied. For instance, for modeling phenomena such as earthquakes, a calibration at the level of a 2,475-year event has been adopted; for extreme events whose materialization might occur on the site of a nuclear power plant, this figure is 409 years; while for flood infrastructure, we speak of a 100-year event.

The End of the Stationarity Assumption

At the outset of the twenty-first century, Milly et al. (2008), in a widely noted article titled "Stationarity Is Dead: Whither Water Management," concluded: "Systems for management of water throughout the developed world have been designed and operated under the assumption of stationarity." At the time, in 2008, they valued the global investment in water infrastructure, designed on the basis of models employing the stationarity condition, at US$500 billion. They described the concept of stationarity, as employed in hydrology over the preceding several decades of the twentieth century, as: "the idea that natural systems fluctuate within an unchanging envelope of variability."

Milly et al. go on to state that this envelope of natural variability has been exceeded, and formulate their central thesis: stationarity is dead. Pointing to climate change as the principal cause of the breach of this hitherto inviolable condition, they write emphatically: "we assert that stationarity is dead and should no longer serve as a central, default assumption in water-resource risk assessment and planning." Drawing on projections of the average level of global surface water runoff for the year 2050, and comparing these levels to observations from the period 1900–1970, they point to a substantial change — one that cannot be captured within a variance understood in stationary terms. In addition, addressing the relationship between changes at the level of the mean and those at the level of distant quantiles, they argue that "paleohydrologic studies suggest that small changes in mean climate might produce large changes in extremes […]," and, with respect to the resilience of currently constructed safety systems, that "projected changes in runoff during the multidecade lifetime of major water infrastructure projects begun now are large enough to push hydroclimate beyond the range of historical behaviors." At the end, the authors propose adapting existing models so as to enable them to capture the temporal evolution of the probability distribution, together with an estimate of the associated uncertainty.

The authors' closing statement — "stationarity cannot be revived" — provoked, within the hydrological community, a debate on the mortality and immortality of stationarity. This question became the dominant research problem within twenty-first-century hydrology (e.g., Cooley, 2013; Salas and Obeysekera, 2014; Du et al., 2015; Read and Vogel, 2015).

The Return Period Under Conditions of Non-Stationarity

Read and Vogel (2015) examined the usefulness of the concept of the expected return period under conditions of non-stationarity, while retaining the assumption of temporal independence. They undertook an attempt to describe the flow phenomenon precisely under such non-stationary conditions — that is, with non-stationary return periods. They demonstrated that, under such unstable conditions, the concept of the T-year flood changes over time. For instance, the 100-year flood, 50 years from now, will be 1.6 times larger than today's 100-year flood. There is thus no single value corresponding to the 100-year flood. This may, of course, give rise to further interpretive — and consequently communicative — complications, with respect to a return-period definition that already causes considerable difficulty even under stationary conditions. It may be, however, that a time-varying exceedance probability p (that is, put differently, 1/T) renders this new, non-stationary concept of the return period altogether devoid of usefulness. What is more, the very form of the non-stationarity may itself be variable, which further complicates any modeling approach whatsoever.

The Return Period Under Conditions of Temporal Dependence Among Observations

Volpi et al. (2015), in the reverse of Read and Vogel (2015), relaxed the assumption of temporal independence while retaining the assumption of stationarity. They constructed a time-dependent model, characterized by the presence of properties commonly found in phenomena of this kind: (i) persistence, and (ii) clustering. The first property denotes a tendency for the same state to persist over an extended period; the second, a tendency for extreme events to cluster within neighboring periods. Using a model built on these assumptions, the authors tested the usefulness of the return period definition. They conclude: "[…] in the context of time-dependent processes, the return period might not represent an exhaustive measure of the probability of failure, and that its blind application could lead to misleading results." Above all, however, the authors drew attention to the difference in results depending on the definition of the return period adopted. They note that, whereas under conditions of temporal independence both commonly used definitions (the unconditional return period and the conditional return period) are equivalent, under conditions of temporal dependence, different definitions yield different results. In addition, they demonstrate that readings of the classically understood return period, under conditions of temporal dependence, lead to erroneous interpretations, and they propose an alternative measure — the equivalent return period — which serves as the counterpart of the return period under conditions of temporal dependence.

Criticism of the Probabilistic Approach in Hydrology

In the field of hydrological research, a certain turning point, it would seem, can be observed: growing criticism of traditional statistical methods, and a shift toward mechanistic descriptions of phenomena. Over the past two decades, an increasing number of publications have appeared subjecting to criticism the assumption of the random character of the flow phenomenon, and of the floods that result from it. Merz and Blöschl (2008) cite Klemeš's statement: "If more light is to be shed on the probabilities of hydrological events, then it will have to come from more information on the physics of the phenomena, not from more mathematics." The authors go on to state that the hydrological literature has focused excessively on solving technical estimation problems (distribution fitting, parametrization methods), neglecting the wealth of hydrological knowledge in the process. They propose shifting the center of gravity of research, pointing out that there exists a rich diversity of hydrological processes, often specific to a given location, which cannot be fully captured by formal statistical methods.

Dawdy, Griffis, and Gupta (2012), lending support to this line of argument, question the unrealistic assumption of stationarity — that is, the assumption that the future can be predicted by means of a model of the past. At the same time, they point to the insurmountable limitations of traditional statistical models. Despite the many improvements made to such models over time, they remain, they argue, a black box, closed off from knowledge of the physical processes actually taking place.

Milly et al. (2008) point, it would seem aptly, to a necessary change in the philosophy of modeling. A model, they argue, should not be the central point of reference, but merely an aid in synthesizing observations: "Modeling should be used to synthesize observations; it can never replace them."

A New Perspective on the Description of Physical Phenomena

This emerging trend, moving toward a mechanistic approach to the construction of resilience systems, in a certain sense renders the ongoing probabilistic debates moot. Within such a new paradigm, it is no longer necessary to determine the exceedance probability, nor the associated concept of the return period, nor any of the other concepts deriving from it. The recently observed problem of non-stationarity in physical phenomena, and the question of the independence of random variables, cease to be of consequence. Consequently, all attempts to resolve problems of this type — that is, problems whose very definitions were constructed on the plane of statistics, or, more broadly, of probability theory — may likewise be rendered moot.

As can be seen, within the world of natural phenomena — which the scientific community has studied for over a century, and for the description of which the entire, sophisticated statistical apparatus was developed — the fundamental assumptions underlying the models widely applied in this domain are increasingly being called into question. Stationarity and the independence of random variables — axioms indispensable to this statistical architecture — have vanished.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

01

The "hundred-year flood" isn't a natural law — it's a statistical convention built on two fragile assumptions.

02

In 2008, hydrology's own researchers declared these foundations dead.

03

The field's response was to shift from statistics toward mechanics — describing the physical process itself, not just fitting a curve to it.

What is more, the entire probabilistic edifice — the founding idea for problems of this class — is beginning to be widely challenged. Perhaps statistics and its tools are not, after all, the best instrument for describing phenomena of such importance to society. Such is the current state of the debate within this domain of phenomena — one relatively amenable to statistical description: natural phenomena, relatively stable and well understood.

What, then, can be said of the possibility of statistically modeling phenomena that are far less well understood, yet far more dynamic — phenomena that play themselves out on a social substrate, marked by the generic unpredictability of human behavior, in an era of exponentially accelerating civilizational change? ■
