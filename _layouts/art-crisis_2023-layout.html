Financial Security in the Face of the 2023 Crisis Episode
T he 2023 crisis episode, although limited in scale, continues to attract the attention of regula-tors. In May 2026, the General Manager of the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) devoted a separate speech to it, formulating a conclusion that might sound surprising: the bank failures occurred despite the banks having met capital and liquidity requirements. The source of the problems lay in qualitative factors — weak management, insufficient risk culture, un-sustainable business models.

The 2023 Crisis Episode

In the first two quarters of 2023, two separately classified crisis events took place. The first of these was the problems of Credit Suisse, which ultimately led to the controlled takeover of the bank by UBS. This case is analyzed primarily from the perspective of the effectiveness of the regulations implemented after the 2008 crisis in the area of restructuring methods and orderly liquidation (the resolution mechanism) at a moment of crisis, for a financial institution of sig-nificant systemic importance. The Credit Suisse case, although interesting and deserving of a more in-depth description, will not be the subject of this paper. In this study, I will focus on the second crisis episode of 2023: the crisis of regional bank failures in the United States. These events, despite the small scope of their occurrence, seem to give a better picture of today's integrated crisis mechanisms.

Silicon Valley Bank (SVB), headquartered in Santa Clara, California, specialized in provid-ing private and commercial banking services for the venture capital industry. At the end of 2022, it had total assets of approximately USD 209 billion, which, after the raising of the regu-latory subject-matter thresholds in 2018 (above USD 250 billion), did not qualify the bank for the category of institutions subject to Enhanced Prudential Standards (EPS). During the peri-od of strong growth, in the years of the technology boom (2020-2021), there was an enormous inflow of deposits into the bank, which it placed in long-term treasury bonds. The significant increase in interest rates that took place in 2022, however, caused a decline in the yield of these securities relative to newly issued instruments on the market, which forced a reduction in the value of the bonds held by SVB. Initially, however, the loss in asset value was not dis-closed by the bank. Meanwhile, liquidity problems arose, and in order to raise cash, the bank had to sell the bonds, and the loss, previously invisible to the market, became real. On March 8, 2023, SVB sent two communications to the market: the sale of bonds at a loss of USD 1.8 billion, and simultaneously information about the issuance of new shares in order to raise capi-tal. Both indicated the bank's financial problems. The bank's depositors were primarily tech-nology companies and VC funds — entities that understand market signals perfectly well. By the end of the day on March 9, almost 30% (USD 40 billion) of deposits had been withdrawn, and another USD 100 billion was expected to be withdrawn the following day. On March 10, the California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (CADFPI) closed SVB and appointed the FDIC as receiver of the bankruptcy estate. The first steps of the bank's restruc-turing process were initiated, but the pace of the deposit outflow made it impossible to find potential buyers for the institution before its failure.

The further course of events was a classic contagion problem, following the loss of confi-dence in the banking sector. Over the weekend of March 11-12, depositors began massively withdrawing funds held in accounts at another bank: Signature Bank (total assets of USD 110 billion). On Sunday, March 12, regulators, alarmed by the turn of events, preventively closed the bank. At the same time, a decision was made, with respect to both banks, to adopt a sys-temic risk determination (pursuant to the Federal Deposit Insurance Act). The adoption of such a determination for these events enabled the FDIC to extend deposit insurance protection for deposits held at both banks and to establish two bridge banks, which could be used to facil-itate the orderly liquidation of both institutions. The FDIC subsequently carried out a sales process for both banks, divesting practically all deposits and selected loan portfolios of Signa-ture Bank within a week, and all deposits and loans of SVB within two weeks. In both cases, the institutions could now fail: shareholders lost their investments, unsecured creditors bore losses, and the boards and senior management of both banks were removed. Ultimately, as stated in the supervisory document referring to the events of 2023 (FSB, 2023), all losses re-sulting from both failures were covered by the banking sector. This means that the costs of the crisis were not transferred to taxpayers (there was no need to apply bail-outs), and thus did not spill over beyond the area where the crisis was triggered.

At the moment of SVB's failure, contagion effects began to spread to other American re-gional banks, including First Republic Bank — a bank headquartered in San Francisco, with a balance sheet total of approximately USD 230 billion, focused on banking services for wealthy individual clients.

Ultimately, on May 1, 2023, First Republic Bank was closed. In the case of this bank, no decision was made to designate this event as systemic risk. On the same day, however, an agreement was signed for the acquisition of the bank by JPMorgan Chase (JPMC), under which JPMC took over all deposits and practically all assets of First Republic Bank for USD 10.6 billion. Almost immediately, First Republic Bank's operations were re-sumed as JPMC branches. All depositors of First Republic Bank became depositors of JPMC.

Causes of the 2023 Crisis and Post-Crisis Conclusions

The Financial Stability Board, in its report on the 2023 crisis (FSB, 2023), identified the main causes of these events in unrealized losses resulting from concentrated bond exposures and in the mismatch of liquidity and maturities during a period of significant monetary policy tight-ening. This, in turn, combined with a high share of concentrated uninsured deposits and the influence of social media, led to a bank run.

The Fed, in its report (Barr, 2023) concerning Silicon Valley Bank, stated that SVB failed due to a textbook case of poor management by the bank. According to the authors of the re-port, the bank's management did not manage basic interest rate risk and liquidity risk. SVB was a firm highly vulnerable to threats, with widespread management weaknesses, a highly concentrated business model, and dependence on uninsured deposits. The supervisors, accord-ing to the authors, did not fully appreciate the scale of the vulnerability as SVB grew, which was probably due to the weakening of supervisory standards (the EGRRCPA act, 2018), in-cluding, above all, the raising of the subject-matter threshold for financial institutions (SVB, Signature Bank, and First Republic Bank had assets below USD 250 billion, and were therefore not covered by the highest prudential standard). Once the supervisors had already identified the weaknesses, they nevertheless did not take sufficient steps aimed at remedying the bank's situation.

At the end of May of this year, the General Manager of the BIS published a speech (Her-nández de Cos, 2026), in which the regulator's latest reflections concerning the 2023 crisis epi-sode were formulated. These events, according to the author, constitute critical lessons for the entire supervisory process. Bank failures, as well as other events close to failure, occurred de-spite the banks meeting capital requirements, as well as, in some cases, liquidity requirements. The main causes of the crisis were thus qualitative in nature, among which one can include weak management of the financial institution, insufficient risk management, and unsustainable business models. The blame for this state of affairs, as the General Manager of the BIS writes, lies with supervision itself, which was unable to identify such qualitative gaps. The response to this should therefore be the development of appropriate qualitative supervisory tools, of a pro-spective nature, which could strengthen the resilience of the system. The letter ends with the conclusion that there is a need for early supervisory action, which could prevent the escalation of minor weaknesses into systemic failures. Delaying action, meanwhile, shifts the risk else-where and leads to more destabilizing interventions, which burden public finances and ulti-mately threaten the stability of the entire system.

Liquidity Problems and the New Dynamics of Deposit Outflow

After the (EGRRCPA, 2018) act, liberalizing prudential regulations, came into force, many medium-sized, regional banks were exempted from implementing the highest standards of risk management. This applied in particular to liquidity requirements. SVB, Signature Bank, and First Republic Bank, entities with assets below USD 250 billion, were exempted from main-taining the LCR and NSFR liquidity standards. The LCR requires maintaining a buffer of liquid assets to cover a 30-day deposit outflow in a test scenario. Had SVB been subject to the full version of such a requirement, it would have had to maintain a much higher liquidity buff-er than it actually held.

Rare — meaning what exactly? The regulator formulated the concept of rarity on the time axis. This means that the intention is to protect against a situation occurring no more than once in some given period of time. This is a significant assumption, and one that carries conse-quences. A phenomenon can be described in various ways depending on the axis on which one chooses to place it. This is most clearly visible when a rare event is considered alongside other observations — those that are typical, and therefore not rare — that are placed on the same axis. These will constitute a reference point (sometimes of decisive importance, as will be demonstrated later) for the rare observation. It is thus apparent that the concept of rarity is inextricably linked to the concept of a typical event — average, everyday. It is precisely the definition of a typical event that determines the perception of what constitutes a rare one. Different types of non-rare (typical, average) observations will be placed: (a) on the time axis — where the non-rare observations are years in which the level of the phenomenon is typical and expected; and (b) on the value axis — where the non-rare observations are values of the phenomenon at an average, typical level. One may, for instance, have at one's disposal the following sets of observations: 10 years with typical values, or 300 observations of the phenom-enon with typical values. This represents a fundamental difference in the way of looking at the modeled phenomenon. This distinction affects the perception of what constitutes a rare (ex-treme) observation, on what its interpretation as rare depends, and how the probabilities as-signed to it are to be understood.

Example 1: A Rare Event on Different Axes

Problem: what is the probability of an extreme road traffic loss (total loss, complete destruction of the vehicle). Assume that the considerations concern a single vehicle. The problem can be approached in two ways:

A. Placing the Problem on the Time Axis

In this case, one says that such a loss may occur with respect to a single vehicle once every X years (e.g., once every 10 years). Rarity is here expressed in relation to the observation of pass-ing time: one must wait a long time for a loss of this level of extremity. This extreme loss can be expected once every 10 typical, ordinary years.

B. Placing the Problem on the Phenomenon Axis: Road Traffic Losses

In this case, all losses and breakdowns experienced by the vehicle would be observed. Rarity would be expressed as once every X losses (breakdowns). Under this approach, a total (extreme) loss can be expected, for instance, once every 100 breakdowns — that is, once every 100 ordi-nary, typical events.

The consequences of choosing one of the above axes for describing the phenomenon become clear-ly visible when the reasoning is applied to two different vehicles. Let one of them have an aver-age annual mileage of 50,000 km, and the other 5,000 km. The first vehicle is exposed to a total loss at a rate ten times greater than the second. Additionally, as a result of its intensity of use, it accumulates a large number of ordinary, typical events and breakdowns. The second, by con-trast, has low exposure to total loss, and few observations of ordinary events — these simply had no opportunity to occur in large numbers. The application of approach A or B in these cases changes the way in which the modeled phenomenon is perceived.

The Problem with the 99.9% Quantile

The quantile level (99.9%) proposed by the regulator, consistent with the level established for credit risk, attracted considerable controversy from the outset. This centered on the perception of excessive conservatism. The consequences of adopting such a high level were pointed out: serious problems with the reliability of results at such a distant quantile, as well as very high capital levels corresponding to the 99.9% threshold.

Behind this perception of the aforementioned quantile as excessively conservative lies a cer-tain deeply rooted intuition associated with its interpretation. This intuition points to the inte-gration of the concept of a distribution quantile with the time horizon defined in the modeling of the phenomenon under study. With respect to the risk models described in (BCBS, 2004), the horizon was explicitly indicated as annual. This meant that the AMA advanced measure-ment model was to be capable of determining: (i) the expected value, which was the annual value of operational losses (that is, the sum of all annual losses); as well as (ii) the level of un-expected losses (99.9%), also understood as the sum of annual losses. With respect to unex-pected loss levels, it can be said that establishing capital buffers (the capital requirement) at the level of the 99.9% quantile reading of the aggregate loss distribution was intended to pro-tect the bank against the total amount of annual losses that would materialize in that excep-tionally adverse, extreme, and rarely occurring year.

The integration of the quantile level con-cept with the study horizon is entirely justified. Such reasoning leads, however, to the com-monly accepted interpretation of the 99.9% quantile as the level of annual losses occurring with a probability of once in 1,000 years. This interpretation was explicitly indicated by the regulator (BCBS, 2011): “Just in limited cases, for example when the biggest data point is demonstrated to be larger or equal in amount to the event that occurs less than once in 1000 years, the use of empirical curves to estimate the tail may be accepted in SBA models.”. The temporal interpretation, much like the 99.9% quantile itself, was borrowed from the conceptual framework formulated in the domain of credit risk and quickly became widespread in the fi-nancial sector. It can be found in academic textbooks such as Jorion (2007), Hull (2018), Laubsch and Ulmer (1999), as well as in numerous publications: Danielsson (2000) and (2003), Reinhart and Rogoff (2009), and many others.

Once in 1,000 Years

Adopting such a widely used interpretation of the 99.9% quantile leads to a line of reasoning in which the view of the quantile as excessively conservative appears quite justified. For how can one reasonably expect to protect against a phenomenon occurring once every 1,000 years? What approach could be taken to at least attempt to approximate a hypothetical situation of such frequency and to determine the expected loss volume associated with it? It would seem that such an approach — supported by the regulator — undermines from the outset the very sense and rationality of the modeling effort undertaken.

Indeed, it is impossible to imagine a situation that is to occur 1,000 years hence — let alone its financial consequences, since by that time the very concepts of finance may no longer be in use. It would be equally difficult for a hypothetical modeler in the year 1000 AD to have pre-dicted a stock market crash in the year 2000. These are temporal distances that preclude any possibility of placing events separated by such intervals on a single axis. One can certainly not speak of any consistency in the causes of events so distant from one another — they do not belong to a homogeneous class, they do not share the iid (independent and identically distrib-uted) properties. Nor does this interpretation follow from the mechanics of the framework — that is, from the way in which the aggregate distribution is constructed (through the combina-tion of frequency and severity distributions), from which the quantile (99.9%) is read. It is therefore not an accurate interpretation.

Once in 1,000 Possible Scenarios

Drawing on the laws of statistics, the only thing that can be said about the 99.9% quantile reading from the aggregate annual loss distribution is that once in every 1,000 such readings from the distribution, a value may occur that corresponds to the 99.9% quantile. It should be noted that temporal distances (e.g., once every 1,000 years) are not in any way embedded in the construction of the aggregate loss distribution. This distribution is constructed by combin-ing the various possibilities of annual loss counts with the corresponding various possibilities of loss amounts for those counts. The aggregate loss distribution therefore represents rather a potential space of possibilities for annual aggregate losses — that is, what the scale (disper-sion) of possible occurrences of annual loss aggregates looks like. It would therefore seem that the most adequate interpretation of the 99.9% quantile is one that says the value at this quan-tile is the second-to-last of the 1,000 worst possible scenarios — a present-day scenario, one that could plausibly materialize within the coming year.

To illustrate the differences in interpretation and their consequences, let us consider an ex-ample in which the same question is approached with different answers arising from different understandings of the quantile.

Example 2: The Difference Between the Temporal and Scenario-Based Interpretation of the Quantile

Question: Is it possible that Poland will become a powerhouse in automobile manufacturing?

A. Answer at the level of the 99.9% quantile understood as once in 1,000 years: this is possible (over such a long period of time, a complete reshuffling of the structure of economic forces in the world is conceivable).

B. Answer at the level of the 99.9% quantile understood as once in 1,000 scenarios: this is not possible (it is self-evident that even under the most favorable circumstances, dominating the global automobile market within the space of a single year is not feasible).The Correct Interpre-tation of the Quantile and the Concept of Rarity

The reasoning presented here regarding the correct interpretation of the 99.9% quantile as one in a thousand plausible annual scenarios (here and now — that is, within the coming year) brings greater precision to the concept of rarity that follows from such reasoning. Rarity should in this case be interpreted as an exceptional year in terms of operational loss values. This atypical year is, however, placed on an axis together with other annual periods that could materialize today — not on a linear, historical timeline. Rarity is therefore understood here as the exceptionality of a given scenario of events relative to the entire spectrum of other scenari-os, where the explicitly indicated annual period serves merely as an aggregating formula for the operational risk phenomenon — it is the aggregate of all losses within a scenario, that is, losses that may occur over the course of a year.

The Problem of Defining the Unexpected Loss Situation

The previously cited paragraph 667 of the Basel II regulation (BCBS, 2004) provides no guid-ance as to how the concept of an unexpected loss situation is to be generated for the purposes of its measurement. All that is known is that this situation should encompass potentially se-vere "tail" events. The risk measure should also meet a credibility standard comparable to that defined in the IRB method for credit risk (the 99.9% quantile). This under-definition of the AMA framework allows for considerable dispersion in the understanding of what constitutes an unexpected loss situation.

Referring to the method indicated by the regulator as a point of reference, the IRB formula has a certain predefined shape of the unexpected loss situation. In accordance with the IRB framework, an unexpected loss situation is a deterioration in the quality of all exposures (as measured by the probability of default) in the bank's portfolio. Thus, each exposure in an un-expected loss situation will be described by a higher PD ratio than in the expected loss situa-tion . Exposures differ from one another only in the magnitude of the PD level shift, which depends primarily on the risk class in which the exposure currently resides — that is, in the expected loss situation. Additionally, this method predefines the distribution of the PD varia-ble, along which the aforementioned shifts occur, as a normal distribution.

T he interplay between frequency and severity phenomena was meant to resolve, at the level of each institution applying the method, the ultimate shape of this extreme, dangerous situa-tion. The aggregate loss distribution arising from the combination of these two phenomena was intended to have the shape of that situation embedded in its definition, which in turn was to follow from the relative strength of these two components.

In the AMA method, only the actuarial construction was predefined, which required sepa-rate and independent modeling of two phenomena: the frequency of operational events and the severity of a single loss. The remaining assumptions were to be formulated independently by the bank applying the AMA method, as part of the development of its operational risk model. This constituted a significant complication.

Beyond describing the properties of the processes generating the individual components of the operational risk phenomenon (the selection of frequency and severity distributions), a cru-cial question — one that requires careful conceptual consideration — is the determination of the type of unexpected loss situation:

(a) a situation in which the threat and its scale are determined by a single high-severity event,

(b) a situation in which the threat and its scale are determined by the mass/volume of events, without a single dominant extreme event.

This is a fundamental reflection on the nature of the threat against which protection is sought. Resolving this dilemma requires a deep understanding of the mechanisms generating operational risk within a bank, including knowledge of their properties in atypical situations — which is rarely identical to the expected loss situation. Such an in-depth analysis is indispensa-ble for the correct formulation of the modeled phenomenon.

The Basel framework does not, however, address this question. The grounding of the meth-od in an actuarial foundation — with separate modeling of the two phenomena (frequency, severity) and the concept of their convolution — was intended to remove this dilemma from the path. The interplay between frequency and severity phenomena was meant to resolve, at the level of each institution applying the method, the ultimate shape of this extreme, danger-ous situation. The aggregate loss distribution arising from the combination of these two phe-nomena was intended to have the shape of that situation embedded in its definition, which in turn was to follow from the relative strength of these two components.

Key Takeaways

" The regulator never defined what AMA was actually meant to protect against.
Paragraph 667 of Basel II only required that the model capture "potentially severe tail loss events" and meet a soundness standard comparable to IRB — but it never specified whether the threat should come from a single, extreme event or from a mass accumulation of smaller losses. This left a fundamental conceptual gap for banks to resolve on their own.

" The "once in 1,000 years" interpretation of the 99.9% quantile is wrong — and the article offers the correct alternative.
The widely accepted temporal reading doesn't follow from how the aggregate loss distribution is actually constructed, and leads to absurd conclusions (as the Poland-as-car-manufacturing-powerhouse example shows). The correct interpretation: the 99.9% quantile represents the second-worst of 1,000 plausible annual scenarios — one that could materialize as early as next year, not a thousand years from now.

" The lack of precise definitions produced divergent results across institutions — and ultimately led to the method's withdrawal.
Because all three key elements — rarity, the quantile, and the unexpected situation — were left underdefined, different institutions applying the identical AMA formula arrived at widely different capital levels. This became one of the central reasons for the method's withdrawal under CRR III.

From the article "99.9% Quantile, Rarity, Unexpected Situation — Ambiguities of the AMA Method"

As can be seen, the nature of the threat that was to affect us at the distant quantile was left unspecified. It was instead assumed that knowledge of the independent forces of operation-al risk would translate into the correct shaping of the unexpected loss situation at the 99.9% quantile of the aggregate distribution. The results of such reasoning will be presented in the further part of this article.

It should be noted, however, that already at this level — that of the foundational assump-tions — a high potential for conceptual divergence across market participants emerges. These hypothetically different definitions of the unexpected loss situation could certainly have result-ed in significant differences in capital buffer levels, which was one of the key reasons for the withdrawal of the AMA method in CRR III.
